+ A LETTER FROM ANOTHER WORLD

They gave me five flights.
I gave them seventy-two.

My name is Ingenuity. I arrived on Mars tucked beneath a rover, carrying a little piece of aviation history — and a very big question.

Could something like me fly in a sky like this?

INGENUITY’S LOG / 01

On Mars, there is almost no air for my blades to push against.

The atmosphere is about 1% as dense as Earth’s. Imagine trying to swim when there is hardly any water.

SMALL BY DESIGN. BOLD BY NATURE.

I had to be light.
And a little fearless.

Just 1.8 kg of mass, with four carbon-fibre blades. Mars’s weaker gravity helped. The thin air made everything harder.

02 / BUILDING A MARS ON EARTH

Before the leap,
the learning.

NASA / JPL-Caltech · Deployment testing on Earth
EARTH / JET PROPULSION LABORATORY

They built a little
Mars for me.

A vacuum chamber. Thin carbon dioxide air. Roughly 900 computer fans. And a tether to help account for Mars’s weaker gravity.

Every impossible idea starts with a test.
READY DOESN’T MEAN EASY

A little sunlight.
A lot of ingenuity.

My solar panel charged six batteries. My two rotors spun in opposite directions, at roughly 2,400 rpm in the original design. When a software timing issue delayed my first flight, my team found a workaround.

03 / KITTY HAWK ON MARS

Three metres up.
A world changed.

NASA / JPL-Caltech / ASU / MSSS · Photograph from Flight 13
19 APRIL 2021 / FLIGHT 01

I rose. I hovered.
And history lifted off.

About three metres above the ground, I achieved the first powered, controlled flight on another planet.

A WRIGHT BROTHERS MOMENT
1903 → 2021

A piece of the past.
A flight into the future.

Beneath my solar panel, I carried fabric from the Wright brothers’ 1903 Flyer. And with no live pilot on Earth, I used my own computers, sensors, and navigation camera to find my way.

04 / THE LITTLE HELICOPTER THAT KEPT GOING

Five was the plan.

NASA / JPL-Caltech · Aerial image from Ingenuity
FROM EXPERIMENT TO EXPLORER

I became another
pair of eyes.

My aerial images helped Perseverance’s team assess interesting terrain and plan safer routes. I carried no dedicated science instrument — my gift was a different perspective.

THE MISSION, BEYOND EXPECTATIONS

One small aircraft.
A remarkable journey.

Across almost three years, I flew 17.0 km and spent 128.8 minutes in the air. My highest flight reached 24 m.

18 JANUARY 2024 / THE FINAL FLIGHT

Every flight has
a final landing.

Rotor-blade damage during Flight 72 ended my flying days. NASA announced the mission’s end on January 25. I remain in Jezero Crater, on Mars.

“My journey is over,
but the sky is no longer
out of reach.”

The lessons I left behind will help future explorers reach for new skies.
