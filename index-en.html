19 APRIL 2021 · JPL MISSION CONTROL

The room erupted in cheers.

On April 19, 2021, at JPL mission control, Ingenuity project manager MiMi Aung leaped from her chair. After an anxious wait, the data confirmed what the team had hoped for.

“We can now say that human beings have flown a rotorcraft on another planet.”

MiMi Aung’s words, following confirmation of Ingenuity’s first flight.

SCENE 1 · IN THE MARTIAN SKY

One tiny helicopter. A place in history.

As she spoke, data had brought news from a world nearly 300 million kilometers away: a tiny helicopter named Ingenuity had hovered above the Martian surface, in the silent red sky.

Signals take time to reach Earth. The team was confirming a flight that had already happened, rather than watching it live.

Two months earlier…

To understand that moment, let’s go back to the day Ingenuity arrived on Mars.

February 18, 2021. After about seven months traveling from Earth, Perseverance and its little companion reached the Red Planet. The first flight was still to come.

SCENE 2 · A PASSENGER BENEATH THE ROVER

An unusual place to travel.

Tucked away beneath the belly of the Perseverance rover, Ingenuity arrived on Mars as an attached payload. No helicopter had ever been delivered to the surface of another planet before.

Throughout the journey, Perseverance carried and protected the little helicopter. Before Ingenuity could spread its wings, they both had to survive the landing.

SCENE 2 · THE SKY CRANE LANDING

Three cables. One gentle touchdown.

Mars’ thin air meant a parachute alone could not safely land the roughly one-ton rover. After the parachute had done its job, a rocket-powered descent stage slowed the rover further.

Then three nylon cables gently lowered Perseverance onto the red ground, with Ingenuity still attached underneath. This sky crane maneuver kept the rocket engines above the rover, reducing the dust and debris kicked up around it.

Success is never assured.

“Success is never assured, and that’s especially true when we’re trying to land the biggest, heaviest, and most complicated rover we’ve ever built to the most dangerous site we’ve ever attempted to land on.”

SCENE 3 · TWO COMPANIONS, TWO MISSIONS

A big rover. A tiny companion.

Perseverance was a six-wheeled explorer weighing about one metric ton, nearly the size of a car, built to endure the rough ground of Mars.

Beside its “mother,” Ingenuity looked like a lunchbox on four slender legs and had a mass of just 1.8 kilograms. The little helicopter had a different challenge: to fly in the very thin Martian atmosphere.

SCENE 3 · MESSAGES HOME

Far from home. Still heard.

Every message between Ingenuity and Earth had to pass through its “mother,” Perseverance. The rover received the helicopter’s signals and relayed them home through the mission’s communications network.

Depending on the positions of the two planets, a one-way signal could take up to about 22 minutes. Ingenuity could not be flown with a joystick from Earth: once airborne, it had to follow its instructions on its own.

SCENE 4 · FROM HERE, LET ME TELL YOU

I did not begin my life as a hero.

Hello, friend. I’m Ingenuity. Before I ever flew, I was an idea that raised plenty of questions. Was bringing a helicopter to Mars too much of a risk?

Some worried that this little experiment could take time and attention away from Perseverance’s important science mission. In my own story, I might call myself an “uninvited guest.” But my engineers believed I deserved a chance.

“Mother,” “uninvited guest,” and Ingenuity’s first-person voice are storytelling devices, not quotations from NASA.

PART 1 · A NIGHT FAR FROM HOME

When the Sun goes to sleep.

Have you ever missed home when you were far away? I’m so far away that even a message has to cross a vast stretch of space. At night, temperatures where I live can fall to around −90°C — much colder than your home freezer!

The thin atmosphere holds heat poorly. Even by day, sunlit ground can be much warmer than the air above it. When night falls, I have to keep my little electronic parts warm. Luckily, Mum Perseverance is nearby to pass my messages home.

PART 2 · TWO CAMERAS

One eye to navigate. One to share the view.

I have two eyes: a black-and-white one that watches the ground to track my speed and direction, and a color one that sends Mars’ beauty home to Earth.

Rocks and patterns beneath me are my landmarks. To you, they might just be scenery. To me, they help answer a very important question: “How am I moving?”

PART 2 · FLIGHT SIX · 22 MAY 2021

One missed beat in midair.

Once, about 54 seconds after takeoff, a glitch interrupted the images from my navigation camera. One image was lost, and the next ones arrived with incorrect timestamps. Suddenly, I was misjudging my own movement.

I kept trying to correct myself, rocking and tilting more than 20 degrees in midair. What a scare! Fortunately, my control system had enough safety margin to keep me flying until I landed safely.

PART 3 · WHEN DUST HID THE SUN

Some days, courage means waiting.

What do you have for breakfast? I have sunlight. But then a dust storm swept in and dimmed the sky. During the January 2022 storm, the sunlight reaching my solar panel dropped by about 18%.

Less sunlight meant less energy, and I still needed to stay warm overnight. My engineers postponed my flight, checked conditions, and waited for better weather. Moving forward does not always mean taking off right away.

PART 4 · THE ENERGY TO KEEP GOING

Flying is only part of the story.

You might think all my energy goes into flying. In fact, surviving the freezing night takes a large share of my battery power. My electronics have to stay warm enough for me to work again the next day.

The energy needed for flying and heating changes with the weather, the season, and the flight plan. Before each flight, my engineers have to make sure I can still get through the next night.

PART 4 · WINTER AND A MISSED APPOINTMENT

I had only forgotten the time.

Then winter came. Longer nights and less sunlight left my battery unable to keep my computer running through the night. I shut down in the cold, as if I had fallen asleep.

When the Sun woke me the next morning, my clock had reset. I had not forgotten my friends; I had lost track of when to contact Perseverance. After an anxious wait, my engineers asked Mum to listen for longer — and we found each other again.

PART 5 · FLIGHT 72 · 18 JANUARY 2024

The day my wings came to rest.

Then came my final flight. Below me lay smooth sand ripples with too few landmarks for my camera to track. Without a clear sense of my sideways motion, I struggled to land safely.

In the engineers’ most likely reconstruction, I hit the slope of a sand ripple hard. The sudden pitching and rolling overloaded my spinning blades and broke them. As fragile as ice-cream cones in my story, those wings could no longer carry me into the sky.

PART 5 · ANOTHER WAY TO KEEP GOING

Broken wings. Lessons that remain.

Though I could no longer fly, I survived the landing and reestablished contact with Perseverance. Before their final shift with me in April 2024, my engineers sent one last software update.

They prepared me to become a stationary testbed: taking pictures, recording temperatures and equipment performance, and storing data locally after the rover moved out of radio range. Perhaps a future explorer will one day find those gifts of data.

This describes NASA’s April 2024 plan, not a claim that Ingenuity is still operating or communicating with Earth today.

PART 6 · LOOKING BACK

Five planned flights. Seventy-two takeoffs.

Looking back on my journey, I can hardly believe it. I was meant to make up to five test flights. Instead, over almost three years, I took off 72 times and traveled about 17 kilometers on another world.

I began as a doubted experiment and became a scouting eye, helping Perseverance’s team see the terrain ahead. My wings may have stopped, but what we learned is opening the way for future flights. And you — where would you like your dream to take you?
